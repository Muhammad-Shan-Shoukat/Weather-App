Sound familiar? You're not alone
Connecting with your teenager is not always easy. But there are things you can
do to build and maintain a good relationship with your teenager. A relationship
between a teenager and a parent is a two-way street. You cannot control the
behaviour of your teenager. However, you can control your own behaviour, and
by doing so, influence your teenager's behaviour.
Connect is about empowering you to change your behaviour so you can
positively influence your teenager. We'll talk about ways to build your
relationship and communicate with your teenager.
We’ll also look at some common emotional reactions to the pandemic, and how
you can support your teen to cope with strong emotions during this time.

Why the ups and downs?
It is normal for teenagers to experience more extreme ‘ups and downs’ in their
emotions. As a parent, you might find it hard to tell why your teen’s mood and
behaviours change.
Many of the changes in adolescents’ emotions, thinking, and behaviour are due
to rapid brain changes that are a healthy and necessary part of their
development into adults. It might be helpful for you to keep this in mind if you
find yourself getting confused or frustrated about how your teen is acting.
You could also chat with your teenager about the changes happening in their
brain and how this might affect them.

The adolescent brain (click to reveal)
Below are some of the main changes during adolescence:
 Connections between parts of the brain change: Unused connections are
‘trimmed’ away, and other connections become stronger.
 Changes begin towards the back of the brain and move toward the front:
these changes continue into the mid-to-late 20s.
 The front part of the brain (called the prefrontal cortex) is involved in
decision-making, planning, rational thinking, problem solving, and
impulse control. Because this part of the brain is still developing during
adolescence, teens are still learning how to master these ‘executive skills’.
 The ‘emotional centre’ of the brain, called the amygdala, is more
developed than the front part of the brain. This can make teens act
impulsively and based on how they ‘feel’.
 Teenage brains are also wired to seek reward or pleasure. At the same
time, their immature frontal lobes make it hard for teens to foresee the
consequences of their behaviour.
Because of these changes, teens are less able to regulate their emotions and
hence, are more likely to experience stronger emotions.

Emotions during the pandemic
Have you noticed your teen is more emotional during the pandemic?
جذبات
؟ جذباتی و گیا میں زیاد مقابل ک پ ل آپ کا نوعمر بچ ک محسوس کیا کیا آپ نہے ہ ہ ے ے ہ ہ ے ہ ہے ے
All of the above emotions are normal and expected. Teenagers are likely to feel
a range of strong emotions during this time. Your teen’s emotional response will
depend on their age, maturity, their understanding of the pandemic, the
response of adults around them (including you and other family members),
their personality, and their coping skills. Let your teen know that it’s normal
and okay to feel these emotions.
Some teens might express their emotions through challenging behaviours, for
example defiance or acting out. If you’ve found your teen’s behaviour has
changed during the pandemic, keep in mind that this could be their way of
expressing strong emotions. Remember, your teen is trying to navigate all of the
challenges of the pandemic, while also developing the ability to express and
cope with their emotions.

1. Show affection and encouragement
The teenage years are a time when young people try to create their own identity
and want to be more independent. Yet they still want to feel connected to their
families. It's a balancing act - the need to be connected and the need to be
independent from others!

How do you help your teenager with this balancing act?
It's important to regularly show your teenager that they are loved and
respected. But how do I do this now that they are no longer a child?
There is no 'one way' to show love and affection to your teenager. What works
for one teenager, may not work for another. What suits one family may not be
appropriate for another family. It also varies with your teenager's age and
maturity.
Choose ways to show affection that suit you and your teenager.

Don't
Make critical comments. Rather than blame your teenager for not
communicating with you, express your desire to connect with them. Instead
of "You never talk to me anymore!", try "I'd love to spend some time with you.
What would you like to do together?"
Nag at your teenager. If the only thing your teenager hears from you is what
they should be doing, they are less likely to talk to you.
Try to fix everything. Sometimes teenagers don't want advice, they just want to
be listened to. Let your teenager share their world without swooping in to save
them.

It's common for teenagers to experience strong emotions. They might look
overly worried or so angry they look like they are about to explode. In order to
manage their emotions effectively, teenagers need to first learn how to
recognise and understand them. Here are some tips on how you can help your
teenager to do this.
Identify - Label the emotion.
Validate - Acknowledge your teenager's feelings about the situation.
Understand - Explore what has led to the emotion

Identify, validate & understand
Identify
Identifying and labelling the emotion helps your teenager feel heard and it
helps you understand their world better. If you see that your teenager is feeling
a strong emotion, ask about their feelings, e.g. "You look worried" or "It sounds
like you're really angry".
Validate
Acknowledge and show empathy for what your teenager is going
through. Teenagers often feel like no one understands them or has ever
experienced what they are going through. Hearing "I've been there, done that"
can often leave a teenager feeling dismissed. Validating the feelings your
teenager is telling you in conversation will help them feel understood. Try "That
must be really tough" or "What a tricky situation." This helps to show empathy
because it communicates your concern for their feelings.
Understand
Explore what has led to the emotions. Ask your teenager about what has
happened to make them feel this way. You could gently ask: "Is there something
on your mind?", "What's making you so upset?" or "Would you like to talk about
what's just happened?"
Letting your teenager tell you what's going on helps them understand what they
are feeling and to manage their emotions effectively. Giving your teenager time
to talk will be important.

Things to avoid
Reacting back the same way
When we are faced with negative emotions we tend to react back with negative
emotions ourselves. For example, if someone is yelling and shouting at you,
your gut reaction may be to yell and shout back in an effort to defend yourself
or end the conversation. Reacting back the same way can lead to a downward
spiral and everyone ends up feeling (more) upset.
Shutting the emotions down
Because we find negative emotions hard to handle or overwhelming, sometimes
we just want to shut them down. Unfortunately, shutting down expressions of
strong emotions or a sensitive conversation doesn't teach your teenager how to
deal with these feelings or the difficult situations that led to them.
Here are some examples of things to avoid:
Dismissing or trivialising your teenager's emotional responses, even if you
think their concerns are unreasonable or if hearing about these feelings
makes you uncomfortable.
 "Don't be so sensitive."
 "Get over it."
Responding in a way that can lead your teenager to believe that their
emotions are wrong.
 "Why are you crying like a baby?"
 "It can't be that bad."
Responding in a way that can make your teenager think that they are bad
for having certain emotions.
 "You're such a scaredy-cat."
 "You're making everyone else feel miserable."
Remember…
Your teenager is still developing their ability to manage the strong emotions
they are experiencing. Sometimes this means that they may express their
emotions in ways that you do not find acceptable, e.g. yelling in anger,
slamming doors, speaking disrespectfully. When this happens, try your best
to separate their emotions from their behaviours. Remain calm, listen well,
and show concern for how they are feeling (the emotions underlying the
behaviours). Then, when things are calm(er), you can have a chat with your
teenager about the behaviours that you found unacceptable, and discuss ways
they can better express their strong emotions in the future. For more tips on
this, see the modules Raising good kids into great adults and Calm versus
conflict.
